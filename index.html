01 / 11 · AboutBiography

At the nexus of medicine, technology & AI

Dr. Sean Khozin is a physician-scientist and oncologist, internationally recognized for his leadership in leveraging data science, AI, machine learning, and real-world evidence to drive innovation in biomedical research, with a particular emphasis on oncology drug development.

A founding member of the FDA Oncology Center of Excellence, Dr. Khozin escaped the revolution in Iran and found his way to the United States, harmonizing his passion for patients and data into a career spanning startups, the FDA, Johnson & Johnson, and pioneering AI ventures, all while pursuing his love of music.

His work bridges the gap between cutting-edge technology and clinical care, with a focus on harnessing advanced analytics and AI to accelerate drug discovery, optimize therapeutic development, and improve patient outcomes.

Convergence

Where the three overlap, the unit of evidence changes.

Medicine supplies the questions and the ground truth. Technology makes biology continuously measurable at the level of the individual. AI turns those measurements into models that can be interrogated, updated, and acted on.

Together they make the life sciences behave less like a sequence of one-off experiments and more like a learning system: trials that borrow strength from external data without giving up randomization, evidence generated at the point of care and not only in the trial, disease classified by mechanism instead of anatomy, and therapies matched to an individual rather than a population average.

This is the thread through Dr. Khozin’s career, from the clinic to the FDA to Johnson & Johnson, CancerLinQ, and Phyusion Bio: not any single field, but the operating system that connects them.

Information Exchange and Data Transformation (INFORMED) was launched in 2015 at the U.S. FDA with special authorities from the Department of Health and Human Services.

As a technology and data science incubator, INFORMED was designed as a public-private collaboration sandbox to de-risk innovations with transformative potential in biomedical research and therapeutic development.

The effort was instrumental in establishing the foundation for the use of real-world evidence, digital health solutions, and AI/ML in drug development and regulatory decision-making.

The INFORMED Frameworks

INFORMED began from a simple premise: that the FDA, by virtue of its authority over how medicines are tested, reviewed and approved, can act as a catalyst for the entire drug development ecosystem, not only as its regulator. Data sits at the center of that role. By modeling and enabling new approaches, the agency could help the ecosystem move from static, structured datasets toward continuous, real-world evidence from electronic health records, devices and omics, expanding across the four dimensions of big data: volume, velocity, variety and veracity. With data at the core, drug development could then be reimagined, not as a linear pipeline, but as a learning system in three layers: systems biology that joins preclinical and clinical evidence, a learning health system that generates evidence at the point of care, and policy and investment decisions informed by network analysis, causal dynamics and game theory.

Dr. Khozin's research spans regulatory science, clinical trial methodology, and data science, with a focus on modernizing how evidence is generated and applied in cancer drug development.

Precision Oncology and Regulatory Science

As a clinical investigator at the National Cancer Institute, Dr. Khozin conducted clinical research in thoracic oncology and rare tumors, contributing to early-phase trials of molecularly targeted agents and novel combination regimens in lung cancer and thymic epithelial tumors.

This work included studies of IGF-1R-targeted therapy in thymoma, survivin suppression in NSCLC, the identification of ALK rearrangements in rare lung cancer histologies, characterization of germline EGFR T790M mutations, and the use of FDG-PET imaging as a predictive and prognostic tool in thymic malignancies. At the FDA, Dr. Khozin led several landmark accelerated and regular approvals that defined the modern paradigm of biomarker-driven oncology, including ceritinib (ALK-positive NSCLC), erlotinib (EGFR-mutant NSCLC), osimertinib (EGFR T790M NSCLC), and atezolizumab (metastatic NSCLC).

Real-World Evidence and Clinical Trial Design

Dr. Khozin’s research has helped define the methodological foundations for using electronic health records and other real-world data to generate validated clinical endpoints, construct robust external control arms, and support regulatory decision-making in oncology.

His work demonstrated that real-world endpoints can meaningfully correlate with overall survival and be implemented at scale with scientific rigor. He has also advanced hybrid trial designs that integrate randomization with external data sources, developed approaches to improve the interpretation of clinical trials for underrepresented populations, and established practical frameworks for incorporating real-world evidence into both regulatory and treatment decisions.

Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning in Drug Development

Dr. Khozin’s work in AI spans foundation models for oncology, LLM-based clinical trial data analysis, and regulatory frameworks for AI/ML adoption across the drug development life cycle.

At the FDA, he established Information Exchange and Data Transformation (INFORMED), the agency’s first data science and technology incubator, which laid the groundwork for integrating AI and digital health into drug development and regulatory decision-making.

A central focus of Dr. Khozin’s current work is modernizing how tumor burden is measured in clinical trials. In a large FDA-led meta-analysis of more than 14,000 patients enrolled in registration trials, he found that two independent radiologists reviewing the same scans agreed on tumor response classification only about 70% of the time. In ovarian and pancreatic cancers, discordance reached as high as 45%, with striking cases in which one reader determined a complete response while the other concluded progressive disease in the very same patient.

Response · automated

AutoRECIST

AutoRECIST applies AI to automate and standardize tumor response assessment, eliminating inter-reader variability that undermines the reliability of clinical trial endpoints. By replacing subjective radiologist interpretation with algorithmic consistency, AutoRECIST produces reproducible, auditable response classifications at scale.

Burden · continuous

Total Tumor Burden Index (TTBI)

TTBI replaces conventional categorical RECIST endpoints with a continuous volumetric measure of total disease burden. By integrating 3D tumor segmentation across all lesions, TTBI enables earlier and more granular detection of treatment effect, capturing changes that binary response categories miss.

07 / 11 · Financial engineeringScience, finance & policy

New funding models for high-risk, high-reward science

Breakthrough biomedical research is often too risky, too early or too slow for conventional capital, so promising science stalls in the “valley of death” between the lab and the clinic. Dr. Khozin’s work examines new ways to fund high-risk, high-reward research: structures that share risk across many programs, capital patient enough for drug development’s long timelines, and quantitative and AI methods, borrowed from finance, that measure each program’s risk more accurately.

  • Risk

    Share it

    Portfolio structures such as megafunds, financed in part with research-backed bonds, pool many programs, so early science becomes investable even when no single project is.

  • Time

    Extend it

    Long-horizon funds, venture philanthropy and public-private partnerships match capital to the 10 to 20 years it takes to bring a cancer therapy to patients.

  • Evidence

    Measure it

    Quantitative methods from finance and AI models that predict trial outcomes sharpen each program’s odds of success, the input every funding decision depends on.

The economics of cancer innovationRisk · Time · Evidence

When finance, not science, is the bottleneck

Oncology has entered an era in which it is “costlier to discover, harder to develop, and less rewarding to succeed.” Dr. Khozin’s work starts from that premise: many promising therapies fail not because the biology is wrong but because the capital structures around them do not fit the risk, cost and timelines of drug development. His work identifies four structural failures and engineers financing to correct them.

  1. 01

    Risk–return misalignment

    Individual cancer programs can fail more than 95% of the time, a risk profile conventional investors will not hold.

  2. 02

    The valley of death

    Promising preclinical science stalls before first-in-human trials, too early for most investors and too costly for academia.

  3. 03

    Short-term capital, long-term science

    Development takes 10 to 20 years, while most venture funds have ten-year lives and seek returns within five to seven, forcing exits midway.

  4. 04

    Misallocated investment

    Capital chases fashionable areas in boom-and-bust cycles, leaving high-need cancers underfunded.

The economics of a single program

A drug program is worth funding when the profit it is expected to earn, valued in today’s dollars, exceeds what it costs to develop. Net present value (NPV) puts both in a single sum: every year’s net cash flow, discounted back to the present.

NPV = TΣt = 1 CFt ÷ (1 + r)t
CFtNet cash flow in year t

The program’s profit in year t: revenue minus every cost. During development it is negative, because the program spends money and earns nothing; after approval it turns positive as sales arrive.

rDiscount rate

The annual return investors require for the risk they take, also called the cost of capital, written as a decimal (0.10 for 10%). At r = 0.10, $1 earned ten years from now is worth about 39¢ today.

tYears from today

How far in the future a cash flow arrives. Each year’s cash flow is divided by (1 + r) once for every year it lies ahead, so distant profit counts for less than near-term profit.

TLast year of commercial life

The final year the drug earns, usually when its patents expire and generics enter. It equals the years spent in development plus the years on the market.

NPV > 0The threshold

A positive NPV means the program returns more than its cost of capital and is worth funding. Below zero, rational investors pass, however promising the science.

Novel financing frameworks

Dr. Khozin’s work focuses on the following financing structures and how they can be applied to correct those failures. Select one for how it works and how it helps.

  • How it works

    A single vehicle holds a large, diversified portfolio of development programs across stages, targets and mechanisms. It is financed with a layered capital structure: equity at the bottom and tranches of research-backed obligations, bonds repaid from licensing, milestone and royalty income, above it. Because programs that are weakly correlated rarely fail together, the portfolio’s volatility falls far below that of any single asset.

    How it helps

    Turns assets too risky for any one investor into an instrument that pension funds and insurers can hold, lowering the cost of capital, r in the formula above.

  • How it works

    Funds with 15- to 20-year terms, modeled on infrastructure finance, raised from patient capital such as endowments, sovereign funds and insurers. Income from later-stage assets or royalties can support the fund over its life, so early programs are not abandoned when a conventional 10-year venture fund must return capital.

    How it helps

    Removes forced exits midway through development and aligns the duration of capital with the 10 to 20 years a cancer therapy takes to reach patients.

  • How it works

    Foundations and patient advocacy organizations fund development programs directly, structured so that success returns royalties or milestone payments to the funder. Those returns are reinvested in the next programs, as the Cystic Fibrosis Foundation did in backing the therapies that transformed its disease.

    How it helps

    Directs capital to high unmet need that markets underweight, such as pancreatic and esophageal cancer, and creates an evergreen pool of funding.

  • How it works

    Government co-investment, first-loss capital, guarantees or advance commitments that absorb part of the downside of early-stage research, alongside private investors who take the remainder.

    How it helps

    Reduces the effective risk and duration private capital must bear, drawing investment into the gap between promising preclinical science and first-in-human trials.

  • How it works

    A single multi-compound entity in-licenses promising assets from many universities and medical centers and builds the development infrastructure around them, so that scientists can remain in academia while their discoveries advance.

    How it helps

    Converts stranded academic intellectual property into an investable portfolio, sharing risk across compounds and lowering development cost through shared expertise.

  • How it works

    Methods from quantitative finance and machine-learning models trained on clinical-trial data that estimate each program’s probability of technical and regulatory success, and how outcomes correlate across a portfolio. Dr. Khozin’s research with Andrew Lo combined machine learning with tumor-growth models to predict outcomes in advanced lung cancer.

    How it helps

    Gives investors a defensible estimate of the odds of success, the input every financing structure depends on, so capital can be priced to the real risk rather than to uncertainty.

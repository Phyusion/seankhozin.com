Theme 01 · The Simulator’s DilemmaAI · Drug discovery

The Simulator’s Dilemma

Better molecules are not better medicines.

Artificial intelligence can now predict how molecules fit together, read the genome’s regulatory code and write proteins evolution never made. Yet the productivity of drug development has not moved. This theme follows one molecule through the tools of its day and through today’s, and argues that AI’s biggest opportunity is not designing the molecule. It is discovering the human biology that decides whether the molecule works.

  1. 01

    The tools are real

    Structure prediction, genome models and protein language models are a genuine step change in how molecules are engineered.

  2. 02

    The outcomes have not changed

    AI-discovered molecules pass Phase I at 80–90% but Phase II at about 40%, in line with history. Drugs per R&D dollar keep falling.

  3. 03

    The bottleneck is biology

    Fragmented data, labels drawn from human perception, products shipped without clinical proof and how little of human biology is mapped limit what any model can learn.

Part I

A bet on simulation

“Every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it.”

McCarthy, Minsky, Rochester & Shannon · Dartmouth proposal · August 31, 1955

The field began with a wager. If something can be described precisely enough, a machine can simulate it. Seventy years later the same wager has moved into biology: describe the protein, the cell or the patient precisely enough, and drug discovery becomes engineering.

The history of that wager is a reminder that progress is never a straight line. Eighty years of AI include two winters, each following a period when expectations ran ahead of what the methods could deliver.

Part II

The molecule that could not

RG7112 was designed to free the tumor suppressor p53 by blocking MDM2, the protein that holds p53 in check. Many tumors keep p53 silent this way, so releasing it is an elegant idea: switch the cell’s own brake back on.

The molecule was built with the best tools of its day, and it passed every test those tools could set. Step through its development to see where it was stopped.

It hit its target and missed the patient.

Part III

New tools of the trade

“For the very first time in human history, biology has the opportunity to be engineering, not science.”

Jensen Huang, 2023

A decade on, the toolkit looks transformed. Four families of models now cover the steps RG7112’s chemists did by hand.

Predicting how molecules fit together

AlphaFold 3

Predicts the joint structures of complexes: proteins, nucleic acids, small molecules and more.

Abramson et al., Nature 2024

RFdiffusion

A diffusion model for de novo design of protein structure and function, such as new binders.

Watson et al., Nature 2023

Reading the genome’s regulatory code

AlphaGenome

Takes 1 Mb of DNA in and predicts thousands of functional tracks at up to single-base resolution. Matches or beats the best external models in 25 of 26 variant-effect evaluations.

Avsec et al., Nature 2026

Evo 2

40 billion parameters, a 1 Mb context, trained on more than 9 trillion nucleotides. Predicts and designs across DNA, RNA and proteins.

Brixi et al., Nature 2026

Writing proteins evolution never made

ESM3

A multimodal generative language model that reasons over protein sequence, structure and function. It produced a bright new fluorescent protein only 58% identical to known ones: a distance equivalent to about 500 million years of evolution.

Hayes et al., Science 2025

Language models join the lab

TxGemma

Language models (2B, 9B and 27B) that predict therapeutic properties with natural-language reasoning. They match or beat the best generalist model on 64 of 66 tasks. Wet-lab validation is still a future step.

Google DeepMind, 2025

AI co-scientist

A multi-agent system built on Gemini 2.0 that generates novel hypotheses and research proposals.

Google, 2025

Part IV

Would today’s tools catch it?

“It takes ten years and billions of dollars to design just one drug. We can maybe reduce that down from years to maybe months or maybe even weeks.”

Demis Hassabis, 2025

The promise is speed. The first clinical results look encouraging, until they reach the stage where efficacy in patients is tested.

80–90%Phase I success for AI-discovered molecules
~40%Phase II success: in line with historical industry rates

Analysis of AI-native biotech pipelines · Jayatunga et al., Drug Discov Today 2024

Phase I mostly tests whether a molecule is safe enough to dose. Phase II is where it first has to help patients, and that is where the advantage disappears. To see why, run RG7112 through today’s toolkit.

Part V

Why Eroom’s law won’t break

Moore’s law, spelled backward. Since 1950, the number of new drugs approved per billion US dollars of R&D has halved roughly every nine years: about an 80-fold decline, adjusted for inflation, despite huge advances in science, technology and management. AI-designed molecules have not bent the curve yet.

What we know often surprises us

Single driver mutations are the exception: most patients with metastatic non-small cell lung cancer and other advanced cancers may not have tumors with a single driver. And even a true driver depends on context.

Our gold standards can be blunt tools

Animal models are the standard gate between the bench and the clinic. In oncology, they predict human toxicity little better than a coin toss.

Part VI

What’s working today

Engineering at full speed

Where the biology is already understood, AI-assisted design is paying off. The clearest signal is in licensing: China-origin assets now account for a large share of big-ticket deals, built by applying fast, AI-assisted design at full scale to validated targets.

42%China-origin share of large-cap licensing deals ($50M+ upfront), H1 2026
68%Of upfront dollars in those deals

J.P. Morgan, Q2 2026 Biopharma Licensing and Venture Report

These molecules are mostly not first in class. They are better versions of known answers, and no new biological understanding is required to make them.

The quiet wins are in the back office

Ambient scribes

Capturing clinical documentation.

Documents

Document management and drafting of regulatory submissions.

Workflow

Automation across business operations.

These improve efficiency, not biological insight.

Part VII

Why biology isn’t moving

Four obstacles keep AI’s progress in engineering from turning into progress in biology.

  1. 1Fragmentation
  2. 2Arbitrary gold standards
  3. 3Medicine is not SaaS
  4. 4Limited knowledge of human biology

1Fragmentation

Of data. Trials, health records, imaging, omics and outcomes sit in separate silos. A model learns only the slice it can see, and no single slice contains the patient.

Of work. AI itself was the by-product of one conversation across disciplines. In the 1940s and 1950s, neuroscience (McCulloch and Pitts’s neuron as a logic unit, 1943), mathematics (Turing’s work on logic and computability, 1936 and 1950) and computer science (stored programs and the Dartmouth workshop, 1956) were in the same room. Today they are super-specialized. Medicine and AI both need multidisciplinary work, and multidisciplinary data.

Suppose someone invented a biosensor and wanted to use it to make life-and-death diagnoses.

The reasonable answer today: that’s premature. Convene a workshop. Talk to the FDA.

It is the electrocardiogram.

  1. 1887Augustus Waller records the heart’s electrical potential from the skin.
  2. 1903Willem Einthoven’s string galvanometer is ready for use.
  3. 1905On March 22, Einthoven, a physiologist, and engineer Johannes Bosscha transmit an ECG by telephone line, about 1.5 km.
  4. 1924Einthoven receives the Nobel Prize.

Physiology and engineering, working together, moved it quickly into practice. The ECG, the X-ray (a physicist’s discovery turned clinical tool) and MRI (physics, chemistry and medicine combined) all came from medicine and engineering sharing a bench. Each would be hard to develop today, across disciplinary and regulatory barriers.

Hjelm & Julius, J Telemed Telecare 2005 · National Academy of Sciences

2Arbitrary gold standards

Human sensory input is an unreliable instrument. Much of the ground truth AI is trained on comes from people looking at images. In an FDA meta-analysis of 13,677 patients in registration trials, the investigator and an independent radiologist reading the same scans disagreed on tumor response about 30% of the time, and up to 45% in ovarian and pancreatic cancer. Assessments by eye vary within and between observers, in radiology and histopathology alike.

The labels themselves are historical accidents. Consider what “lung cancer” means.

Surgeon’s eye

“Lung cancer”: an early surgical observation of where a tumor sits.

Pathologist’s eye

“Small cell” vs “non-small cell”: what a pathologist sees through a microscope. “Non-small cell” defines a cancer by what it is not.

Underneath

Distinct molecular drivers, grouped together as one disease.

Train AI on these labels and, at best, it learns to reproduce them faster: faster horses, not biological insight.

Khozin et al., FDA meta-analysis, 2017 · Khozin, Clin Transl Sci 2024

3Medicine is not SaaS

Software playbook

Ship it

A regulatory clearance feels like a launch. Technology companies and their investors shy away from clinical trials.

Medical product playbook

Prove it

Medical products need clinical validation. A 510(k) clearance alone does not win clinicians or payers.

The cost of confusing the two is real: Pear Therapeutics, a pioneer of FDA-authorized prescription digital therapeutics, filed for bankruptcy in 2023. Meanwhile the regulatory door to simulation has opened faster than industry has used it.

4Limited knowledge of human biology

1–5%?

A plausible share of human biology that has been mapped: a sliver. And that counts only the known unknowns. What is truly known may be far less.

The human genome encodes about 20,000 proteins, many still uncharacterized, and research keeps returning to the ones already well studied. By one systematic ranking of how much is known about each protein family, nearly a quarter of human families (23%) still sit at the very bottom. A simulator cannot simulate what no one has measured.

Rocha et al., PLOS Biol 2023

Theme 02 · From Organs to AlgorithmsClassification · Pathology · AI

From Organs to Algorithms

Cancer is still named for where it grows. The treatments that work best follow what drives it.

For more than a century, cancer has been classified by the organ it starts in and how it looks under a microscope. Molecular profiling has since shown that tumors in different organs can share the same driver, while tumors in the same organ can be strikingly different. This theme traces how the names arose, what they cost patients, and how AI could build a classification from mechanism up, provided it is not trained to copy the old labels.

  1. 01

    The names are historical accidents

    Anatomy and histology were the best instruments of their time. They were never designed to predict which drug will work.

  2. 02

    Mechanism already wins

    In FDA lung cancer trials, about 4 patients needed treatment for one to benefit when patients were selected by molecular profile, against 11 without selection.

  3. 03

    Algorithms must learn biology, not labels

    Models trained on inherited categories learn to reproduce them faster. The opportunity is in features no human defined.

Part I

How cancer got its names

Every classification is a record of the instruments available when it was written. Cancer’s was written in layers: first by what could be seen, then by where it was found, then by what its cells looked like through a lens.

Each layer added real knowledge. But despite sequencing, transcriptomics and single-cell proteomics, the system in daily use is still anchored in anatomical location and histology: a framework rooted in historical practice rather than in what is now known about the biology. It constrains how far the newest diagnostic tools can be exploited, and how precisely therapies can be aimed.

Khozin, Clin Transl Sci 2024

Part II

The promise of 100%

The founding premise of precision medicine is simple: the right therapy for the right patient, at the right time and dose. Taken literally, it implies that every correctly matched patient should respond.

In practice, responses in trials and in the clinic spread across a wide range, and a substantial share of patients with metastatic disease gain little or nothing. The argument of this theme is that the principle is not wrong. The matching is. Classification based on organ and microscope too often fails to find the right patient.

“What if the ‘noise’ is the signal? What if what we have been calling variation is actually heterogeneity: meaningful biological differences that determine whether a patient thrives or dies on a given therapy?”

Sean Khozin · The Ghost in the Statistical Machine, 2026

The average patient, the figure every trial reports, exists nowhere except in the aggregate. A classification that lumps different diseases together widens that average and hides the patients a drug could have helped.

Part III

Defined by what it is not

“Lung cancer” records an early surgical observation of where a tumor sits. “Non-small cell” records what a pathologist did not see through the microscope. Together they define a disease by negation, and group malignancies with distinct molecular drivers, immune environments and resistance mechanisms under a label that encodes none of that.

Where it sits

“Lung”: the organ, from the surgeon’s map.

What it is not

“Non-small cell”: a negative, from the microscope.

What drives it

EGFR, ALK, ROS1, BRAF, KRAS, RET, MET, HER2, NTRK… each a different disease, each with different drugs.

Labels drawn from human perception are also unstable. In an FDA analysis of 13,677 patients in registration trials, two expert readers looking at the same scans disagreed on tumor response about 30% of the time.

When these inherited categories become training labels, AI does not escape them. Most AI imaging work still correlates what a model learns with existing “gold standard” criteria, which reinforces the same assumptions the old categories suffer from.

“When these inherited categories become training labels for foundation models, the models do not simply inherit their limitations; they amplify them.”

Sean Khozin · The Simulator’s Dilemma, Revisited, 2026

Part IV

The numbers say it already

Across 30 lung cancer trials that supported FDA decisions from 2003 to 2017, researchers counted how many patients had to be treated for one to benefit. The answer depended less on the drug than on whether the patients had been selected by the biology of their tumor.

The same pattern shows up in response rates. With doublet chemotherapy in morphologically defined non-small cell lung cancer, responses hover around 30%. In molecularly defined settings they routinely reach 50–90%.

Khozin, The Simulator’s Dilemma, Revisited, 2026

And when machine-learning models were trained on patient-level data from 17 FDA-submitted lung cancer trials, biomarker status was the strongest predictor of response, progression-free survival and overall survival. But single biomarkers had limited predictive value, especially PD-L1 for immunotherapy. The authors concluded that going further requires composite, multiomic signatures: a classification built from many measurements at once.

Siah, Khozin, Wong & Lo, JCO Clin Cancer Inform 2019

Part V

Lung cancer, split by split

“‘Lung cancer’ has decomposed into a taxonomy of molecular species.”

Sean Khozin · The Ghost in the Statistical Machine, 2026

One name has been coming apart, approval by approval. Drag through the years to watch “non-small cell lung cancer” divide into diseases defined by their drivers.

Once a disease is defined by its driver, a small study can be decisive. In March 2016, FDA approved crizotinib for ROS1-positive lung cancer on the strength of about fifty patients from a single-arm study, in which independent review found a 66% response rate. The evidence fit in a modest summary table. The biology did the rest.

Khozin, Fifty Patients, 2026

Part VI

Re-sort the tumors

Group tumors by organ and each group mixes different diseases, while the same driver turns up in many groups. Group them by driver and tumors from different organs line up behind a shared target.

Regulators have already started to classify this way. In May 2017, FDA approved pembrolizumab for any solid tumor with high microsatellite instability or mismatch-repair deficiency: the first approval defined by a biomarker rather than an organ. It came during Dr. Khozin’s tenure at FDA, at a time when moving away from anatomy was, in his words, “at best an ambiguous concept for many stakeholders.” Approvals for NTRK fusions, BRAF V600E, RET fusions and HER2 followed.

The idea was visible even earlier. Imatinib, built for the BCR-ABL kinase in chronic myeloid leukemia, extended to gastrointestinal stromal tumors once KIT came into view: the first hint that a targeted drug could cross disease boundaries when the biology is shared.

Khozin, Unearthing Mechanistic Foundations of Oncological Diseases with AI, 2023 · Khozin, The Discipline of Precision, 2026

Part VII

What algorithms can see

In 2016, in the second game against Lee Sedol, AlphaGo played Move 37: a stone placed where centuries of human strategy said it did not belong. It looked like a mistake. It was a solution humans had not conceived.

Dr. Khozin calls these non-obvious, or “alien,” constructions: solutions that are better than the human ones and that lie outside the set of answers humans would think of. In pathology and imaging, the alien constructions are features: patterns in tissue and scans that predict biology and outcome, which no human ever named.

Already in the literature

Reading slides as an expert does

Expert-level grading

A deep-learning system for Gleason grading of prostate biopsies agreed with expert uropathologists and outperformed general pathologists.

Bulten et al., Nat Med 2022

Faster, surer diagnosis

AI assistance cut diagnosis time by up to 62% for plasma cell identification and 33% for lymph-node metastases, and reduced diagnostic uncertainty and requests for extra tests by over 60%.

Huang et al., Nat Biomed Eng 2024

Finding what no one named

New prognostic markers

Patterns in the stromal environment of colorectal tumors predicted survival; slide analysis found a distinct subset of endometrial cancers with markedly worse outcomes.

Kather et al., PLoS Med 2019 · Darbandsari et al., Nat Commun 2024

Features shared across cancers

Four imaging features common to three malignancies, each with its own molecular character and prognostic meaning.

Wu et al., Nat Mach Intell 2021

Molecules from morphology

Mutations from a stained slide

Microsatellite instability and POLE mutations detected from routine H&E slides in colorectal cancer; across cancers, morphology predicted specific mutations.

Gustav et al., NPJ Precis Oncol 2024 · Fu et al., Nat Cancer 2020

Expression, and response

Genome-wide tumor mRNA expression inferred from slides, then used to predict response to targeted and immune therapies.

Hoang et al., Nat Cancer 2024

Structure in space

Tumor microregions

Across 131 tumor sections from six cancer types, spatial transcriptomics, multiplexed imaging and single-nucleus sequencing revealed distinct microregions, each with its own genetic clones. Treatment-resistant subclones tended to form larger, metabolically active microregions.

Mo et al., Nature 2024

Imaging beyond the eye

A lung-cancer screening model outperformed experienced radiologists in detection; image-based deep learning identified patients likely to respond better to radiotherapy.

Ardila et al., Nat Med 2019 · Lou et al., Lancet Digit Health 2019

Trained models also leave behind a rich mathematical representation, the latent space, in which data is compressed and sorted into clusters humans cannot form unaided. That is where new disease categories are most likely to be found: if the representation is explored for itself, rather than forced back onto the old labels.

Khozin, Unearthing Mechanistic Foundations of Oncological Diseases with AI, 2023

Part VIII

Part IX

What it will take

A computationally powered classification has to clear the same bar as any other medical claim. The obstacles are known.

  1. 01
    Standardized, integrated data

    Robust multi-center datasets that link molecular, imaging, pathology and clinical records.

  2. 02
    Interpretability

    Models whose reasoning can be examined in biological terms.

  3. 03
    Clinical validation

    Evaluation guidelines and prospective trials, not only retrospective benchmarks.

  4. 04
    Ethics

    Attention to bias in who is represented in the data that defines a disease.

  5. 05
    Teams and infrastructure

    Clinicians, researchers and AI specialists working together, with the computing to match.

Khozin, Clin Transl Sci 2024 · Elemento, Khozin & Sternberg, NEJM AI 2025

“A mutation list is not a disease model. A pathway diagram is not an intervention strategy. A biomarker is not a mechanism unless the biology makes it one.”

Sean Khozin · The Discipline of Precision, 2026

Classify the biology, not the organ.

When the training data captures proxies, the model learns proxies. Built on mechanism instead, a classification can find the right patient, and deliver what precision medicine has always promised.

The Broken Instrument

What we measure determines what we can find.

For twenty-five years, nearly every solid-tumor drug approved on tumor-based endpoints has rested on one instrument: a convention for pressing a ruler against a shadow on a CT scan. Two expert readers applying it to the same images disagree about a third of the time. This theme follows what that disagreement does once it enters a trial: how it shrinks real effects, discards information, turns borderline patients into coin flips, and quietly shapes which drugs get developed. And why, for the first time, the instrument can be replaced.

  1. 01

    The ruler is a convention

    RECIST certifies drugs, but almost no one uses it to treat patients. Its authority comes from agreement, not validation.

  2. 02

    Noise has a direction

    Random disagreement does not cancel out. It shrinks observed effects, inflates trial sizes and tilts results toward false negatives.

  3. 03

    The substrate is replaceable

    Volumetric, continuous, machine-read measurement can now be the same in the trial and in the clinic.

Part I

A ruler against a shadow

RECIST (Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors) works like this. A radiologist picks up to five target lesions, no more than two per organ, and measures each along its longest diameter. The diameters are summed and compared with the baseline sum. Shrink by 30% or more and the patient has a partial response. Grow by 20% or more, and at least 5 mm, and it is progressive disease. Anything in between is stable disease. Disappearance is a complete response.

The convention has the unusual property of being both ubiquitous and invisible. It is how drugs are certified, not how they are administered. Its history explains why it looks the way it does.

Part II

An old warning

The problem was measured before the modern criteria existed. In 1976, sixteen experienced oncologists each measured twelve simulated tumor masses with their usual clinical methods. Unknown to them, some masses were identical in size. Comparing their measurements of identical masses showed how many “responses” measurement error alone would produce.

7–8%“Responses” from error alone at a 50% shrinkage criterion
19–25%“Responses” from error alone at a 25% criterion

Moertel & Hanley, Cancer 1976

The authors advised investigators to expect a 5–10% response rate from human error in measurement alone. Half a century later, the instrument has changed its geometry, from two diameters to one, but not the fact that a human hand holds the ruler.

Part III

Two readers, one scan

Registration trials have a useful quirk. Every scan is read twice: by the investigator and by a blinded radiologist at a central facility, each unaware of the other. In 2017, Dr. Khozin and colleagues at FDA pooled a decade of these paired reads.

13,677Paired assessments
69.5%Agreed on the response category
>40%Disagreement in hard-to-measure cancers such as pancreatic and ovarian

Explore the dataThe full reader-agreement matrix, cell by cell →

In the language of measurement theory, this corresponds to a weighted kappa (agreement beyond chance) of roughly 0.30 to 0.55: between “fair” and “moderate” on the scale most often used to grade it. That is what one expects from a diagnostic tool in early development, not from the instrument on which oncology drug development rests.

Landis & Koch, Biometrics 1977 · Khozin, The Broken Instrument Beneath the Evidence, 2026

The standard defense is procedural. Disagreement is random, spread evenly across both arms of a randomized trial, so the comparison stays fair and the p-value stays honest. That is partly true. It is also incomplete, and many cancer drugs are advanced on single-arm studies with no comparison arm at all.

Part IV

The attenuation

When random misclassification enters a comparison of two proportions, the difference between them does not stay the same. It shrinks. If each patient is misclassified with probability m, the observed difference between arms is the true difference multiplied by (1 − 2m). The result goes back at least to Irwin Bross in 1954, and it is rarely invoked in trial design.

The direction is the point. Random measurement error biases observed effects toward the null. At the level of the instrument, that produces a systematic excess of false negatives over false positives. A trial that misses statistical significance is not necessarily a trial of an inactive drug. Some fraction of negative trials in solid-tumor oncology are trials of active drugs measured with an instrument that could not see them.

No one knows the size of that fraction. The instrument erases the evidence of what it failed to detect.

Part V

Four boxes for a continuous world

Underneath the categories, the biology is continuous: the percent change in tumor burden can take any value. RECIST turns it into four ordered boxes, and response rate collapses those into two. A four-level category carries at most two bits of information; a yes-or-no outcome carries at most one. A continuous measurement carries several times more.

A drug that shrinks every patient’s tumors by a quarter, a remarkable result implying a real mechanism, can post a response rate of zero, the same as no treatment. Trial designs that keep tumor change continuous, with a control group, were proposed precisely to stop discarding that information.

Karrison, Maitland, Stadler & Ratain, J Natl Cancer Inst 2007 · Shannon, Bell Syst Tech J 1948

Part VI

A coin flip at the cutoff

Take a patient whose tumor burden truly fell by 28%: two points short of a partial response. If readers’ measurements vary by about ten percentage points, as the disagreement data suggest, how often will this patient be called a responder?

Forty-two reads in a hundred call it a partial response; fifty-eight call it stable disease. At the boundary, the category is close to a coin flip, and the reported effect depends on how many of those flips land on the side that counts.

The instability does more than add noise. Because response rate cuts sharply at minus 30%, sponsors are rewarded for drugs that produce deep responses in a few patients, and drugs that produce shallow but consistent shrinkage across many are structurally disfavored. The shape of the oncology pipeline, enriched for responses the human eye can see, follows from the endpoint. It selects for what the instrument can detect, not necessarily for what prolongs life.

Part VII

An instrument only trials use

Community oncologists, who treat most patients with cancer, do not sum diameters. They read narrative radiology reports (“stable appearance of the right upper lobe mass”) and combine them with clinical judgment. When Dr. Khozin showed the reader-disagreement data to Richard Pazdur, FDA’s longtime head of oncology, Pazdur was not surprised. “You know, Sean, I never liked RECIST,” he said. What he trusted most was the “lipstick sign”: a frail patient returning to clinic after a few cycles of treatment, color back in her face, wearing bright red lipstick.

23%Patients for whom RECIST progression could be determined from health-record data (6 of 26)
87%With a real-world progression event using radiology- and clinician-anchored approaches

Griffith, … Khozin, … Miksad, Adv Ther 2019

So the variable on which approval rests is absent from the data routine care generates, and trial results cannot be checked against practice on the same scale.

“Who has validated ORR as a surrogate?”

A true surrogate, in Ross Prentice’s 1989 definition, must capture the treatment’s effect on the outcome that matters, so that once the surrogate is known, no residual effect remains. Response rate has never been validated this way. When Dr. Khozin, early in his FDA tenure, described response rate as a surrogate, Robert Temple, then deputy center director for clinical science, asked the question above. It had no answer.

0.89R², response rate vs progression-free survival, across FDA lung cancer trials
0.09R², response rate vs overall survival
0.08R², progression-free vs overall survival

Blumenthal et al. (incl. Khozin), J Clin Oncol 2015 · 14 trials, 12,567 patients

Across FDA approvals from 2009 to 2014 that rested on surrogate endpoints, more than half of accelerated approvals had no formal analysis of how well the surrogate tracked survival; where analyses existed, correlations were mostly low. Even the strongest precedent shows the difficulty: pathological complete response in early breast cancer predicts survival powerfully for individual patients, especially in aggressive subtypes, yet the pooled analysis could not validate it as a trial-level surrogate.

Prentice, Stat Med 1989 · Kim & Prasad, Mayo Clin Proc 2016 · Cortazar et al., Lancet 2014 · Khozin, The Misnomer of Surrogate Endpoints in Oncology, 2025

Part VIII

Machines, readers, references

Run one chest CT through a measurement algorithm a thousand times and it returns the same number a thousand times. Run it through a generative AI system and it returns a distribution of reports, mostly in agreement. Give it to a thousand radiologists and they disagree on the response category roughly 30% of the time.

Algorithm

Identical output on every run. Perfectly reproducible, which is not the same as correct.

Generative AI

A distribution of reports. Reliability can be measured run by run.

Radiologists

About 30% disagreement on the category that decides whether a drug stays in development.

Two principles follow. Reliability is necessary for validity, but not sufficient: a system can return the same wrong answer every time, and consistency reads as confidence. And when machines are judged against a panel of clinicians, the panel itself has to be measured. A device that agrees with a three-person panel 85% of the time sounds deficient until one learns the panelists agree with each other only 78% of the time. Machines and readers should report reliability on the same scales: kappa for categories, intraclass correlation for continuous measures.

Khozin, A Thousand Runs, 2026 · the 85% and 78% figures are an illustration

Part IX

Replacing the substrate

In 2009, the RECIST working group considered moving to volumetric or functional measurement and concluded that there was not yet enough standardization or evidence to abandon the diameter. That condition has changed. Volumetric segmentation is now routine, imaging foundation models are trained on more scans than any radiologist reads in a career, and automated assessment can run at scale in trials and in community care.

The regulatory path will take time: prospective comparison with RECIST, integration across imaging systems, agreement on validation. None of these barriers is technical.

Replace the measurement, not the statistics.

What we measure determines what we can find. What we can find determines what we develop. What we develop determines who is treated. The ruler pressed against the tumor for a generation is not the only ruler available. It is simply the one we inherited.
